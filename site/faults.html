The Wasatch fault in a minute

The Wasatch fault zone runs about 220 miles from Malad City, Idaho, to Fayette, Utah. It's a normal fault: the valley drops down and the mountains rise, a few meters at a time, in large earthquakes thousands of years apart. That's how the Wasatch Range got so tall and the valleys so deep.

The fault breaks in pieces called sections, each of which tends to rupture on its own. The six central sections show repeated large earthquakes in the last several thousand years:

Not just the Wasatch

The West Valley fault zone (the Granger and Taylorsville faults) cuts across the middle of the Salt Lake Valley and likely moves together with the Salt Lake City section. Utah's largest recorded earthquake, a magnitude 6.6 in 1934 in Hansel Valley, north of the Great Salt Lake, broke the ground surface. Southern Utah has its own active faults, including the Hurricane fault.

Reading the colors

Faults are colored by how recently they've moved. Red faults have moved in the last 15,000 years, which in geologic time is yesterday. Those are the ones scientists watch most closely. An older fault isn't necessarily safe; it may just have a longer gap between earthquakes, or less study.
